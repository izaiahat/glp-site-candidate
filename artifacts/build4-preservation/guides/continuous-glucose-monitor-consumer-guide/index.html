A CGM guide to readings, intended use, diagnostic limits, cost, and data sharing

By the Clinic Scout Editorial Team · Updated July 23, 2026

Sources checked 2026-07-23 · Prices and terms can change.

Quick answer

A continuous glucose monitor estimates glucose in interstitial fluid over time and shows readings and trends through a receiver or compatible device. Consumers should check the exact system’s intended-use population, safety information, alert and confirmation instructions, total cost, data sharing, and privacy terms. A CGM trace is not a stand-alone diagnosis.

The sensor, display, and interpretation are three layers

A CGM system gathers information through a sensor and presents readings and trends through a receiver or compatible device. A separate app or wellness service may add scores, meal labels, coaching, or predictions. Those layers should not be treated as one source of truth. Identify which claims come from the device information and which come from software built around it. A compelling dashboard can add an interpretation that the underlying authorization does not make.

This layered view helps when products are sold as subscriptions. The consumer may be paying for sensors, access to an app, professional services, or some combination. Ask which company provides each part and what stops working if the subscription ends. Device function, data access, and coaching access may have different terms. Knowing the layers prevents a feature of the app from being mistaken for a property of the sensor.

Hardware and software can have different update cycles. A sensor may stay the same while an app changes its score or recommendation rules. Note the app version or date when a surprising interpretation appears. A new display does not necessarily mean the underlying measurement changed, and an old article may describe features the current service no longer offers.

Intended use belongs ahead of lifestyle promises

CDC explains CGMs in the context of diabetes management, and FDA provides device-level information and safety context. The exact system’s intended-use population matters. A company marketing a monitor for general wellness should not imply that every claim is part of the device’s authorized use. Ask whether the statement comes from the FDA device record, peer-reviewed research, or the company’s own interpretation of customer data.

The FDA clearance of an over-the-counter CGM does not make every use, conclusion, or population automatically supported. Clearance concerns a defined device and intended use. A seller should identify the exact model and describe the relevant boundaries. “FDA cleared technology” is incomplete when it appears beside a broader promise about metabolism, weight, energy, or food optimization without support for that specific outcome.

Intended-use wording should be read for the exact model rather than inferred from the category. Different systems may target different users or purchasing routes. A general article about CGMs can explain the technology while remaining unable to establish that one device is appropriate for a particular person. Product-level questions need product-level information.

A CGM reading is not the same as a diagnostic blood test

NIDDK describes validated blood-test categories used to assess diabetes and prediabetes. A CGM estimates glucose in interstitial fluid over time, which is a different measurement context. This guide does not compare numerical values or tell readers how to respond to a reading. It preserves the basic boundary: continuous monitoring data should not be presented as a stand-alone diagnosis merely because the graph contains many points.

More data can improve visibility without answering every medical question. A person may see recurring patterns and want an explanation. The appropriate next step is not for a consumer article to name a condition. Preserve the device, date, circumstances, and original display, then discuss the concern with a qualified professional. A wellness company’s automatic label should not replace that conversation.

Diagnostic restraint matters even when a graph looks persuasive. Dense data can create an impression that no further test or context is needed. NIDDK’s material reminds readers that diagnostic blood tests are defined tools. A qualified professional can determine how monitoring observations relate to those questions; a wellness dashboard cannot appoint itself as the answer.

Alerts and confirmation instructions are system specific

A useful device review asks what alerts the exact system provides, what limitations the manufacturer states, and when official instructions call for confirmation or another action. This article does not reproduce those instructions because they must come from the current information for the exact system. A comparison page that says simply “real-time alerts” may hide important differences in feature availability, compatible devices, or intended use.

Check which features require a phone, receiver, internet connection, or paid app. Determine what happens when data are missing or a sensor is unavailable. Those are practical product questions, not medical advice. The answers help a consumer understand whether the advertised experience matches the equipment and services included in the purchase. A demo screen should not be assumed to represent every supported device or plan.

Feature comparisons should distinguish unavailable, optional, and included. An alert shown in a demonstration may require a compatible phone or paid plan. A receiver may be sold separately. Ask the company to identify dependencies in writing so the advertised experience can be priced and understood before the first sensor is activated.

Wellness scores can outrun what the sensor measures

A service may convert CGM data into a food score, metabolic score, or daily grade. Ask how the score is calculated, which inputs it uses, and what health claim the company attaches to it. A private score can organize behavior without being a validated diagnosis or predictor. The company should distinguish an educational feature from a clinical conclusion and disclose whether the method has been evaluated for the population it markets to.

Be wary of moral language around ordinary data. A “bad” spike or “perfect” day can encourage anxiety and overinterpretation. The graph reports a signal within the system’s limits; it does not grade character. This guide does not tell consumers what pattern is desirable or what food choice to make. It asks product writers to use neutral, accurate descriptions rather than turning every movement into a warning or a sales opportunity.

Score explanations should reveal whether the app uses only CGM readings or adds meals, activity, sleep, questionnaires, or other information. A change in any input can move the score without a change in the sensor data. Consumers need that distinction before treating the number as a direct reflection of glucose or of health more broadly.

The real price includes access after the first sensor

A trial price may cover one sensor while the ongoing experience requires replacements, an app plan, shipping, a receiver, or coaching. Compare the total over a defined period and ask which features remain available without renewal. If a clinician review is offered, determine whether it is included, optional, or separately billed. A low entry amount is not a complete cost when the dashboard shown in the advertisement requires a different plan.

Insurance and eligibility claims should be checked for the exact consumer and product. A general statement that CGMs are “covered” does not establish what a particular plan will pay. Likewise, an over-the-counter purchase does not guarantee reimbursement through another benefit. Ask the payer or administrator directly and keep estimates separate from final decisions. This guide makes no price, eligibility, or coverage promise.

Replacement timing and access interruptions belong in the cost discussion. Ask how a failed or delayed sensor is handled, who provides support, and whether the app remains usable between devices. These are service questions rather than instructions for managing glucose. They determine whether the subscription description matches ordinary disruptions a consumer may encounter.

Glucose data can travel farther than expected

Read the privacy notice before linking a sensor or uploading health information. Identify the company collecting raw readings, the company operating the app, service providers receiving data, and any stated use for analytics or marketing. A medical appearance does not answer those questions. The consumer should know how to request access, export, or deletion where applicable and whether ending the subscription changes data retention.

Sharing features also deserve context. A dashboard may allow a user to invite a clinician, coach, family member, or friend. Determine what each person can see and how access is revoked. This guide does not assess a particular company’s security. It treats data flow as part of the product decision because a device can be useful while the surrounding service asks for permissions a consumer does not expect.

Privacy review should include exported data, not only live access. Determine the format available to the user and whether the record remains available after cancellation. A proprietary chart can be difficult to discuss elsewhere if the underlying readings cannot be retrieved. Data portability is a practical product feature with consequences for continuity.

Choose questions the exact system can answer

Before buying, record the model, intended-use population, official device information, required receiver or phone, alert features, confirmation instructions, sensor duration stated by the manufacturer, ongoing supplies, app requirements, total recurring cost, and data terms. Then write the purpose for considering it. If the purpose is diagnostic, treatment related, or tied to symptoms, a qualified professional should guide the question rather than a wellness score.

Clinic Scout is a publishing service and the publisher claims no clinical credentials. We do not interpret CGM traces, advise insulin or food decisions, diagnose diabetes or prediabetes, or recommend a device. CDC, FDA, and NIDDK sources support a careful distinction among diabetes management, device authorization and safety, and validated diagnostic blood tests. Consumers can use that distinction to evaluate the product, dashboard, contract, and privacy terms without turning a stream of readings into a medical verdict.

The purchase question should match the device’s job. Someone seeking diabetes management support, general observation, or an answer to unexplained symptoms is asking three different questions. This guide does not choose a device for any of them. It directs medical questions to a qualified professional and leaves consumer comparison focused on the exact system and contract.
